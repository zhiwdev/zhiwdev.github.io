Becoming an AI-Native Team

Draft

Every team needs its own memory, and cross-team work needs one that Legal has signed off on.

My personal notes live in Obsidian as plain markdown: what I read, what I learn, what I decide and why. An agent reads and writes that layer with me, filling gaps and keeping it organized. It's close to the pattern Andrej Karpathy described as an LLM wiki: a markdown knowledge base that an agent keeps current.

The payoff shows up when I need the reasoning behind an old decision, not just the conclusion. I ask the agent and it comes back with the reasoning, because the reasoning is in my notes, linked to where it came from. Once in a while it also notices that two of my notes disagree and asks me which one is right.

That works for one person. The question I keep coming back to is what it looks like for a team. Picture asking your team's agent why a pilot got killed last quarter. It can't tell you, and a better model wouldn't help. The reason lives in someone's head, or in a chat thread from months ago that nobody can find. The team has no memory.

The context foundation

Most teams aren't AI-ready yet, and many don't know it. Their institutional knowledge (why decisions were made, how the work actually gets done, what was tried and dropped) is scattered across pods, inboxes, and people's heads. Put an agent on top of that and it fails for lack of context.

We've learned this lesson once already, with data. Nobody runs shared analytics on CSVs sitting in someone's inbox, so we built a data foundation. Agents need the same thing one layer up. I call it the context foundation.

The shape matches, but what goes into it is different from your data catalog. A catalog tells you what the data means. The context foundation tells you why a decision was made and how the work gets done. Documenting that is as important as unifying the database.

An agent with perfect data and no context is a brilliant amnesiac.

So what is an AI-native team? My test is what the team does differently on an ordinary Tuesday:

  1. Decisions get written down as markdown by default.
  2. The agent reads the team's layer, and writes to it through approval.
  3. A new hire's first week runs on the same layer the agent reads.

Why this time is different

"We tried knowledge management. We have the wiki nobody updates to prove it."

Fair. The sharper version is that this is documentation hygiene with a new name. Hygiene failed because the reader was a person browsing and the writer was a person typing. Both sides paid a lot and got little back. What's new is the reader. An agent that retrieves at machine scale changes the return on every document. A note no person would ever browse is still worth writing if an agent pulls it up again and again.

The second difference is the one I wrote about in my last post: the agent writes as well as reads. Markdown is plain text that people and agents can both read and edit, and every change shows up in a diff. That means upkeep, the thing that killed every wiki, can move to the agent. It only works with verification, which is built into every layer below.

A layer for every team

The obvious move is one context layer for the whole company. I think that's the wrong shape, especially in pharma. The walls between commercial, medical, and R&D are legal requirements, and one big layer ignores them. An agent with total recall is also an agent with total gossip.

My view: every team gets its own context layer, and cross-team work gets a shared layer that Legal has approved. The company's part is the set of rules every layer follows.

TeamTeam layers

Each team owns its layer: its decision records, how its work gets done, what it tried and dropped. Only people with a need to know can read it. This is where most of the knowledge lives.

Tie each layer to a function, such as vaccines commercial analytics, rather than to a box on the org chart. Teams get merged and renamed; the work usually continues. Write down who inherits a layer when its team goes away.

SharedCross-team memory

Cross-team projects, like a launch that touches commercial, medical, and market access, need one place where the whole decision lives. Without it, each team keeps its own slice of the story and nobody sees the full reason.

This layer is legal-approved. Only what's cleared to cross the walls goes in, every cross-team decision has a named owner, and Legal signs off on what gets shared.

CompanyCommon rules

This part is the set of rules every layer follows: one decision-record format, the same trust tiers, permissions, audit, retention, and legal holds. Legal needs one way to find and preserve records across every team layer; separate layers still answer to one set of controls.

The agent works with the user's permissions. It reads your team's layer plus whatever shared records you're cleared for, and nothing else. A decision moves outward in steps:

Inside a team layer

This is a retrieval and workflow pattern, not a model-building project. You don't need an AI team. You need pipes and permissions. Each team layer has three parts, and the guardrails in the first come before anything else.

Part 1Capture

Guardrails come before a single transcript lands. Decide what the agent may never see before you feed it anything, and keep legal, HR, and other sensitive meetings out by default. In a regulated company, consent to record, retention schedules, and legal holds apply to the layer just as they do to the source.

Then run ETL for context. Extract the raw material (recordings, threads, decks), transform it (transcribe, summarize, reduce it to decisions and reasons), and load it as markdown linked back to its source.

Part 2Interchange

Markdown is the common format everything converts into. The original records stay the evidence. The markdown is the index and the summary, and only approved decision records count as the team's answer. An agent's draft never feeds the next draft until a person has approved it, so one bad summary can't turn into accepted fact.

Part 3Read-write loop

The agent maintains the layer: drafting updates, linking notes, flagging what's stale. It writes freely to a draft space, and anything published needs a person's approval. Key claims link back to the moment they came from, which gives you traceability, though not a guarantee of correctness. Sampling audits catch the rest, so when the agent gets something wrong, someone finds it and undoes it.

The unit is the decision

Software teams solved part of this years ago with architecture decision records: one short file per decision, kept next to the code. I'd borrow the idea for every team. Each decision gets one record, in the same format across every layer, so an agent with access to two layers reads both the same way. Here's the template I'd start with:

# Decision: [short title]

Date:         YYYY-MM-DD
Status:       draft | approved | superseded by [link]
Owner:        [name or role]
Layer:        team | shared (legal-approved)

## What we decided
One or two sentences.

## Why
The reasoning, in plain words.

## What we considered and dropped
- Option, and why not

## Sources
- [meeting, thread, or deck, with a link to the moment]

Incentives: where every attempt died

Why would anyone write the docs? Every earlier attempt at knowledge management died on that question.

My answer: nobody should have to write them as a chore. The docs should be a byproduct of work that's already happening. The agent drafts the decision record from the transcript and a person gives it a quick review. The captured path has to be the easiest path, and the work has to be recognized and measured like any other output.

This plan does create a labor bill. Someone has to run the sampling audits, curate the layer, handle disputes, and, for the shared layer, get Legal's sign-off. Reviewing a draft is much cheaper than writing from scratch, though, and a lot of that review already happens informally today, every time someone says "ask the person who was there."

The rubber-stamp worry is real, too. Approvals nobody reads are theater. The sampling audits are the answer: check enough publishes that the agent and the people both know someone is looking.

The ten-decision test

Before you build anything, get a baseline. Pick ten decisions your team made last quarter. For each one, ask your agent (or a new hire) why it was made, and count how many answers come back with a source. That's your score. Run it again after a month.

Here's how I'd start:

  • Before week 1Clear it. Agree with Legal, Privacy, and IT on what may be captured, what stays out, who has to consent to recording, and how long records are kept. Run the ten-decision test.
  • Week 1Capture. Pick one team. Turn on transcription where it's approved and start archiving threads and decks. Almost no behavior change; the layer grows on its own.
  • Week 2Point the agent at it. Let people ask what was decided about X and why Y changed. There's nothing new to learn; it's one more place to ask.
  • Week 3Let evidence do the convincing. My bet is that the first time it answers something nobody could find, the holdouts come around. Start the byproduct loop: the agent drafts decision records, people review.
  • Week 4Make it official. Rerun the ten-decision test, recognize the curators, and write the layer into how the team works. Then pick the first cross-team project and set up its shared layer with Legal.
Nobody adopts a system on a promise. They adopt it after it answers a question they couldn't answer themselves.

Keeping it honest at scale

Disputes. When two accounts of the same decision collide, and with several people's recollections in a layer they will, the layer shouldn't pick one. It tags the dispute, cites both sources, and sends it to the record's owner to settle.

Suggested text from here down. Keep, edit, or replace with your own.

Rollback. Every agent edit can be traced to who approved it and undone if it turns out wrong.

Access. A record inherits the permissions of its sources. If you couldn't open the original email, you can't read its summary either.

Retention. Records follow the same retention and legal-hold rules as the material they came from, in every team layer and in the shared one.

The short version

The model is the easy part now. What most teams are missing is memory: a record of why things happened, kept current by the agent and checked by people. Give every team its own layer, give cross-team work a shared one that Legal has approved, and hold all of them to one set of rules. Skip that and you get a brilliant amnesiac.

This post describes my personal setup and a general pattern. It doesn't describe any employer's systems, programs, or plans. All views are my own.
