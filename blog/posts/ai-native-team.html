Becoming an AI-Native Team

Draft

An agent is only as good as what your team remembers.

My personal notes live in Obsidian as plain markdown: what I read, what I learn, what I decide and why. An agent reads and writes that layer with me, filling gaps and keeping it organized.

The payoff shows up when I need the reasoning behind an old decision, not just the conclusion. I ask the agent and it comes back with the reasoning, because the reasoning is in my notes, linked to where it came from. Once in a while it also notices that two of my notes disagree and asks me which one is right.

The question I keep coming back to is what this looks like for a team. Picture asking your team's agent why a pilot got killed last quarter. It can't tell you, and a better model wouldn't help. The reason lives in someone's head, or in a chat thread from months ago that nobody can find. The team has no memory.

The context foundation

Most teams aren't AI-ready yet, and many don't know it. Their institutional knowledge (why decisions were made, how the work actually gets done, what was tried and dropped) is scattered across teams, pods, inboxes, and people's heads. Put an agent on top of that and it fails for lack of context.

We've learned this lesson once already, with data. Nobody runs shared analytics on CSVs sitting in someone's inbox, so we built a data foundation. Agents need the same thing one layer up. I call it the context foundation.

The shape matches, but what goes into it is different from your data catalog. A catalog tells you what the data means. The context foundation tells you why a decision was made and how the work gets done. Documenting that is as important as unifying the database.

An agent with perfect data and no context is a brilliant amnesiac.

Some fragmentation is on purpose. In pharma, the walls between commercial, medical, and R&D are legal requirements. I'm talking about the other kind: knowledge that's scattered out of habit.

So what is an AI-native team? My test is what the team does differently on an ordinary Tuesday:

  1. Decisions get written down as markdown by default.
  2. The agent reads the shared layer, and writes to it through approval.
  3. A new hire's first week runs on the same layer the agent reads.

Why this time is different

"We tried knowledge management. We have the wiki nobody updates to prove it."

Fair. The sharper version is that this is documentation hygiene with a new name. Hygiene failed because the reader was a person browsing and the writer was a person typing. Both sides paid a lot and got little back. What's new is the reader. An agent that retrieves at machine scale changes the return on every document. A note no person would ever browse is still worth writing if an agent pulls it up again and again.

The second difference is the one I wrote about in my last post: the agent writes as well as reads. Markdown is plain text that people and agents can both read and edit, and every change shows up in a diff. That means upkeep, the thing that killed every wiki, can move to the agent. It only works with verification, and that's what Layer 3 is for.

The map: three layers

This is a retrieval and workflow pattern, not a model-building project. You don't need an AI team. You need pipes and permissions.

Real teams will build these layers in whatever order they can. My one rule: the guardrails in Layer 1 come before anything else.

Layer 1Capture

Guardrails come before a single transcript lands. The layer has to respect the trust boundaries the organization already has, so set up permissions first and capture second. An agent with total recall is also an agent with total gossip. Decide what it may never see before you feed it anything. In a regulated company that also means consent to record, retention schedules, and legal holds apply to the layer just as they do to the source.

Then run ETL for context. Extract the raw material (recordings, threads, decks), transform it (transcribe, summarize, reduce it to decisions and reasons), and load it as markdown linked back to its source.

Layer 2Interchange

Markdown is the common format everything converts into. It has no permissions or governance of its own; Layer 1 handles that. The practical rule: all new capture goes in as markdown, and the archive stays where it is, readable but no longer the source of truth.

Layer 3Read-write loop

The agent maintains the layer: drafting updates, linking notes, flagging what's stale. Trust tiers keep this honest. The agent writes freely to a draft space, and anything published to the shared layer needs a person's approval.

Key claims link back to the moment they came from. That gives you traceability, though not a guarantee of correctness. What catches errors is containment: tiers plus sampling audits, so when the agent gets something wrong, someone finds it and undoes it.

Incentives: where every attempt died

Why would anyone write the docs? Every earlier attempt at knowledge management died on that question.

My answer: nobody should have to write them as a chore. The docs should be a byproduct of work that's already happening. The agent drafts from the transcript and a person gives it a quick review. The captured path has to be the easiest path, and the work has to be recognized and measured like any other output.

This plan does create a labor bill. Someone has to run the sampling audits, curate the layer, and handle disputes. Reviewing a draft is much cheaper than writing from scratch, though, and a lot of that review already happens informally today, every time someone says "ask the person who was there."

The rubber-stamp worry is real, too. Approvals nobody reads are theater. The sampling audits are the answer: check enough publishes that the agent and the people both know someone is looking.

Here's how I'd start:

  • Before week 1Clear it. Agree with Legal, Privacy, and IT on what may be captured, who has to consent to recording, and how long records are kept. In a regulated company this step isn't optional.
  • Week 1Capture. Turn on transcription where it's approved and start archiving threads and decks. Almost no behavior change; the layer grows on its own.
  • Week 2Point the agent at it. Let people ask what was decided about X and why Y changed. There's nothing new to learn; it's one more place to ask.
  • Week 3Let evidence do the convincing. My bet is that the first time it answers something nobody could find, the holdouts come around. Start the byproduct loop: the agent drafts notes from transcripts, people review.
  • Week 4Make it official. Track a few numbers (how often the agent answers with a cited source, how long a "why did we decide X?" question takes, how many notes the audits find stale), recognize the curators, and write the layer into how the team works.
Nobody adopts a system on a promise. They adopt it after it answers a question they couldn't answer themselves.

Making it work at org scale

Governance. Who curates? Who owns a conflict? When two accounts of the same decision collide, and with several people's recollections in the layer they will, the layer shouldn't pick one. It tags the dispute, cites both sources, and sends it to the document owner to settle.

Rollback and an audit trail mean every agent edit can be traced to who approved it and undone if it turns out wrong.

Access. The layer inherits the permissions of its sources. If you couldn't open the original email, you can't read its summary either.

Retention. Notes follow the same retention and legal-hold rules as the records they came from. When the source is deleted on schedule, its note goes with it.

The short version

The model is the easy part now. What most teams are missing is memory: a shared, governed record of why things happened, kept current by the agent and checked by people. Build that and your agent has something to work with. Skip it and you get a brilliant amnesiac.

This post describes my personal setup and a general pattern. It doesn't describe any employer's systems, programs, or plans. All views are my own.
