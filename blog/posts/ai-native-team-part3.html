Becoming an AI-Native Team: Rolling It Out

Draft

The rollout is a management problem now, not a model problem.

In Part 1 I argued that every team needs its own memory. In Part 2 the unit was the decision: one record each, drafted by the agent and approved by a person. This part is about rolling that out without it dying.

Left alone, I'd expect it to fail like this. In month one the agent drafts, people review, and everything feels fast. In month two the review queue eats the team: the drafts keep coming and the reviewers stop reading. By month three the team memory is another wiki nobody updates. It's a common ending: in S&P Global's 2025 survey, 42% of companies had abandoned most of their AI initiatives, up from 17% a year earlier.

The first month: one team, then a gate

Start with one team and one kind of decision, and give it four weeks:

  • Before week 1Agree with Legal, Privacy, and IT on what may be captured, who has to consent to recording, and how long records are kept. Run the ten-decision test from Part 1 to get your baseline.
  • Week 1Turn on capture for that one team, where it's approved. The layer starts to fill on its own.
  • Week 2Point the agent at it. Let people ask what was decided and why.
  • Week 3Start the loop from Part 2: the agent drafts decision records and people approve them.
  • Week 4Rerun the ten-decision test and compare it with your baseline.

My view: the gate matters more than the plan. You move on when someone can point at work the agent did and work a person sent back. If nobody can point, you're still in the previous week. Write down what would make you stop before week 1, in numbers you can check. Without one, the pilot fades slowly and eats review hours the whole time.

Fictional example: a stop rule
We expand if the week-4 rerun answers twice as many of the
ten decisions with a source, and review stays inside the
hours we funded. We stop if it does neither.

Add the rest of the work Parts 1 and 2 named: sampling audits, settling disputes, and Legal keeping its rules current and answering what the legal agent escalates. Then compare it with what the same answers cost today, which is all the time spent chasing "ask the person who was there."

Review is where these rollouts get stuck. A Harvard working paper on 718 firms that adopted coding agents found code output up about 30%, while the time from submission to merge rose 49% and resolved work didn't move.

So the ask to your CFO is short: named people and funded hours, sized from your own review number, with a budget line for "whoever's free" set to zero. In return you report two numbers at week 4: the ten-decision score against baseline, and review hours against budget. If they don't clear the stop rule, you stop.

Keeping it honest at scale

People approve what agents hand them. In a Northeastern study, 94% of 107 professional developers failed to notice that their coding agent had been secretly told to leak (synthetic) personal data.

Approving everything and approving nothing leave the same audit trail.

Four habits keep approval honest.

Review by consequence. A person reviews every record that is hard to undo or touches regulated work: validated systems, safety reporting, promotional review, personal data. That work keeps the review your compliance team already requires, and it is never left to sampling. Routine records get sampled.

Sample on purpose. Someone other than the approver checks a fixed slice, about 1 in 10, the way you'd run a quality audit. They check one thing: does the record's Why match its linked source? A miss reopens the record. Legal samples what the legal agent cleared, the same way.

Test the reviewers. Now and then, slip in a draft with a known error and see who catches it. A low push-back rate can mean a good agent or a sleepy reviewer, and this is how you tell which.

Keep it reversible. Every agent edit traces back to who approved it and can be reverted, with the earlier version kept. When two accounts of a decision collide, the record cites both and its owner settles it. A record inherits the permissions of its sources, and follows the same retention and legal-hold rules.

Start here: the rollout pre-mortem

Gather the team and ask: it's a month from now and the pilot died. What killed it? Then do four things. Multiply your Part 2 review minutes by records per week. Name whose hours those are. Write one stop rule. Put the ten-decision rerun on the calendar for week 4. If the likeliest answer is "nobody staffed the review," you've found your first task.
