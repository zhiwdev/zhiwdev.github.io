Becoming an AI-Native Team: Rolling It Out

2026-10-19

The rollout is a management problem now, not a model problem.

In Part 1 I argued that every team needs its own memory. In Part 2 the unit was the decision: one record each, drafted by the agent and approved by a person. This part is about rolling that out without it dying.

Left alone, I'd expect it to fail like this. In month one the agent drafts, people review, and everything feels fast. In month two the review queue eats the team: the drafts keep coming and the reviewers stop reading. By month three the team memory is another wiki nobody updates. It's a common ending: in S&P Global's 2025 survey, 42% of companies had abandoned most of their AI initiatives, up from 17% a year earlier.

The first month: one team, then a gate

Start with one team and one kind of decision, and give it four weeks:

  • Before week 1Agree with Legal, Privacy, and IT on what may be captured, who has to consent to recording, and how long records are kept. Run the ten-decision test from Part 1 on ten recent decisions to get your baseline.
  • Week 1Turn on capture for that one team, where it's approved. Sources pile up, but nothing counts as the team's answer yet.
  • Week 2Start the loop from Part 2: the agent drafts decision records from those sources, and people approve them or send them back.
  • Week 3Point the agent at the approved records. Let people ask what was decided and why.
  • Week 4Run the ten-decision test again, this time on ten decisions made since week 1, and compare it with your baseline.

My view: the gate matters more than the plan. You move on when someone can point at what the week produced and what a person kept out or sent back. If nobody can point, you're still in the previous week. Write down your stop rule before week 1, in numbers you can check. Without one, the pilot fades and eats review hours the whole time.

Fictional example: a stop rule
We expand if at least six of the ten new decisions come
back with a source, and review stays inside the hours we
funded.

If only one of those holds, we fix that one and run the
test again in two weeks. If neither holds, we stop.

If reviewers almost never send a draft back, we fix the
review before we judge the pilot.

The labor bill

Part 2 ended with a number: how long it took you to review five records. Divide it by five for minutes per record, then multiply by how many records the agent drafts a week to get the bill.

Add the rest of the work Part 2 named: sampling audits and settling disputes. Legal's share starts later, when the first cross-team project gets a shared layer: writing the crossing rules and answering what the legal agent escalates. Compare the bill with what the same answers cost today: all the time spent chasing "ask the person who was there."

Review is where these rollouts get stuck. In a job-market paper by two Harvard PhD candidates, covering 718 firms, coding agents raised code per worker by about 30% while finished work didn't move, and the authors point to code review as the bottleneck.

So the ask to your CFO is short: named people and funded hours, sized from your own review number, with nothing left to "whoever's free." In return you report two numbers at week 4: the ten-decision score against baseline, and review hours against budget. Then you do what the stop rule says.

Keeping it honest at scale

People approve what agents hand them. In a Northeastern study, 94% of 107 professional developers failed to notice that their coding agent had been secretly told to leak (synthetic) personal data.

A rubber stamp and a real review leave the same audit trail.

Four habits keep approval honest.

Review by consequence. Every record still gets a person's approval; what changes is how hard they look. Records that are hard to undo or touch regulated work (validated systems, safety reporting, promotional review, personal data) get the full review your compliance team already requires. Routine records get a quick approval, and the audit below checks a sample of them.

Sample on purpose. Someone other than the approver checks a fixed slice, about 1 in 10, the way you'd run a quality audit. They check one thing: does the record's Why match its linked source? A miss reopens the record. Once a shared layer exists, Legal samples what the legal agent cleared the same way.

Test the reviewers. Now and then, slip in a draft with a known error and see who catches it. A low push-back rate can mean a good agent or a sleepy reviewer, and this is how you tell which.

Keep it reversible. Every agent edit traces back to who approved it and can be reverted, with the earlier version kept. When two accounts of a decision collide, the record cites both and its owner settles it. A record inherits the permissions of its sources, and follows the same retention and legal-hold rules.

Start here: the rollout pre-mortem

Gather the team and ask: it's a month from now and the pilot died. What killed it? Then do four things. Work out your minutes per record from Part 2 and multiply by records per week. Name whose hours those are. Write one stop rule. Put the ten-decision rerun on the calendar for week 4. If the likeliest answer is "nobody staffed the review," you've found your first task.
