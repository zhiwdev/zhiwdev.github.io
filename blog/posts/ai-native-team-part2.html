Becoming an AI-Native Team: The Unit Is the Decision

Draft

Write down decisions, one at a time, and let the agent do the drafting.

In Part 1 I argued that every team needs its own memory. The next question is what goes into it. Most teams reach for documentation: pages, decks, wikis. I think that's the wrong unit.

A document mixes many decisions and is written for someone browsing. Six months later nobody can tell which parts still hold. When your agent is asked why the deal fell apart in the final week, what it needs is the one decision, the reason behind it, and where that reason came from.

One decision, one record

Software teams worked this out years ago with architecture decision records: one short file per decision, kept next to the code. I'd borrow the idea for every team. Each decision gets one record, in the same format in every layer, so an agent with access to two layers reads both the same way.

A documentA decision record
HoldsMany decisions, mixed together HoldsOne decision
AnswersWhat we know AnswersWhat we decided, and why
When things changeEdited in place When things changeA new record supersedes it; the old one stays
OwnerWhoever touched it last OwnerA named person

The template

Here's the template I'd start with. It fits on one screen on purpose.

# Decision: [short title]

Date:         YYYY-MM-DD
Status:       draft | approved | superseded by [link]
Owner:        [name or role]
Layer:        team | shared
Cleared by:   legal agent (rule [id]) | Legal, after escalation

## What we decided
One or two sentences.

## Why
The reasoning, in plain words.

## What we considered and dropped
- Option, and why not

## Sources
- [meeting, thread, or deck, with a link to the moment]

Two fields do most of the work. Why is the part that usually lives only in someone's head. Sources links each claim to the meeting, thread, or deck it came from, so anyone can check the agent's reading. Here's what a filled-in record looks like:

Fictional example
# Decision: Walk away from the renewal with Client A

Date:         2026-03-14
Status:       approved
Owner:        Head of Partnerships
Layer:        team

## What we decided
Stop negotiating and let the contract end at term.

## Why
Their final ask put pricing below our floor for this segment,
and matching it would have reset terms for similar accounts.

## What we considered and dropped
- Matching the price for one year: sets a precedent for
  similar accounts
- Escalating to their executive sponsor: tried; their
  position didn't change

## Sources
- Deal review meeting [link to the moment]
- Pricing committee thread [link]

Nobody writes them by hand

Every earlier attempt at knowledge management died on one question: why would anyone write the docs? My answer is that nobody should start from a blank page. The agent drafts the record from the source, and a person reviews it.

How a record gets made
01
Source
Meeting, thread, or deck
→
02
Agent draft
Fills the template, links each claim to its source
→
03
Person reviews
Edits, approves, or rejects
→
04
Approved record
Joins the team's layer; the source stays as evidence

The sources stay the evidence. The record is a summary that points back to them, and an agent's draft never feeds the next draft until a person has approved it, so one bad summary can't harden into accepted fact.

The labor bill

This does create work. Someone has to review drafts, run sampling audits, and settle disputes. Reviewing a draft is much cheaper than writing from scratch, though, and a lot of that review already happens informally today, every time someone says "ask the person who was there."

The rubber-stamp worry is real, too. Approvals nobody reads are theater. Sampling audits are the answer: check enough approved records that the agent and the people both know someone is looking. Part 3 covers how.

Start here: your first five records

Go back to your ten-decision test from Part 1. Take the decisions your agent couldn't explain, pick the five that matter most, and write a record for each. Let the agent draft from whatever source you can find, and time how long your review takes. Keep that number. It's the honest cost of a team memory, and Part 3 starts from it.
